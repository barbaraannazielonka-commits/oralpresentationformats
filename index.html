Public speaking, decoded

Own the room, whatever format you're handed

A presentation isn't just what's on the screen — it's a format with its own rules.

Slides + speaker

The classic PowerPoint

This is the format most teachers assign, and it's easy to get wrong by cramming every fact onto the screen. A strong PowerPoint has a shape — a beginning, a middle, and an end — and your slides support what you say instead of replacing it.

  • Slide 1 — TitleYour name, your surname, and the topic of your presentation. Nothing else. This is your handshake, not your content.
  • Slide 2 — ContentOnly keywords and images belong on a slide — never long sentences. This is a speaking activity, not a writing one: the slide is a prompt for you, not a script for the audience to read.
  • Last slide — SourcesEvery source you used, formatted in APA style and listed alphabetically by author. It shows your research was real and lets your teacher check your work.

A sources slide isn't enough on its own. Listing your sources at the end only proves you did the research — using them out loud proves you understood it. When you state a fact or statistic, say where it came from in the moment: "According to the WHO..." or "A 2023 study found...". That's what turns a bibliography into evidence.

What makes it work

  • You could explain each slide with it turned off
  • Consistent fonts, colors, and layout throughout
  • You rehearsed the timing out loud, not just in your head
  • Eye contact with the audience, not the screen
  • Sources formatted correctly in APA style, not just pasted URLs

What sinks it

  • Paragraphs of text the audience has to read silently
  • Reading your slides word for word
  • Clip art, clashing colors, ten different fonts
  • A sources slide that's just raw links, not APA citations
20 slides · 20 seconds each

Pecha Kucha

Japanese for "chit-chat," Pecha Kucha is a strict format: 20 image-only slides, each on screen for exactly 20 seconds, advancing automatically whether you're ready or not. Total runtime: 6 minutes 40 seconds. The clock — not you — is in charge.

What makes it work

  • Slides are pictures only — you supply every word out loud
  • You've timed your script against the real 20-second cutoff
  • Each slide covers exactly one beat of the story
  • Short, punchy sentences instead of long explanations

What sinks it

  • Writing a script for 30 seconds when you only get 20
  • Text-heavy slides — there's no time to read them anyway
  • Freezing when a slide changes before you're done talking
  • Never rehearsing against an actual timer
Recorded, face on camera

The face-to-camera video

No live audience, no slides to hide behind — just you, talking straight into the lens. Showing your face matters: it's what makes the presentation actually yours, and it forces the same eye contact and delivery you'd need live.

What makes it work

  • You look at the camera lens, not your own face on screen
  • Good lighting in front of you, not behind you
  • Quiet room, clear audio — this is what actually gets marked down most
  • You speak in a natural voice, not a flat script-read
  • You did a take two when the first one felt stiff

What sinks it

  • Eyes glued to notes the whole time, never the camera
  • A dark room or a window glaring behind you
  • Background noise, echo, or a shaky handheld shot
  • Reading in a monotone because it's "just a recording"
One idea, told as a story

The TED-style talk

TED talks aren't built around slides at all — they're built around a single idea worth spreading, delivered as a story with a beginning, a turn, and a payoff. Slides, if you use them, are barely there.

Watch real talks at ted.com ↗

  • The hookOpen with a question, a moment, or a surprising fact — never "today I'm going to talk about."
  • The storyGround your idea in something concrete: an experience, an example, a person. Ideas stick when they're attached to a story.
  • The insightSay plainly what you want the audience to understand or believe by the end.
  • The takeawayClose with what the audience should think or do differently now. Land it — don't trail off.

What makes it work

  • You could give this talk with the slides turned off entirely
  • Real eye contact and movement, not a fixed spot on the floor
  • Your voice changes pace and volume instead of staying flat
  • One idea, memorable, repeated at the start and the end
  • Evidence named out loud — who said it or where it's from — not just implied

What sinks it

  • Trying to cover five ideas instead of committing to one
  • Slides doing the talking instead of you
  • Memorizing word-for-word and freezing if you lose your place
  • No clear ending — it just stops
One visual board, explained live

The poster presentation

Here the board doesn't move — you do. You create a single visual poster and stand next to it, walking visitors through your work out loud and answering questions as they come, instead of advancing through a fixed script.

Keep the sources off the poster itself. Print them as a separate handout in APA style that visitors can take with them. That keeps the board visual and uncluttered while your citations stay complete and available to anyone who wants to check them.

What makes it work

  • Title and key visuals readable from a few feet away
  • Diagrams, images, and charts doing the work, not paragraphs
  • You can explain any part of it without reading off the board
  • Comfortable fielding questions instead of running a script
  • Sources on a separate APA-style handout, not on the poster

What sinks it

  • Dense blocks of small text no one can read from a distance
  • Standing silently while visitors read it themselves
  • No clear order, so no one knows where to start
  • No handout, leaving no way for anyone to check your sources
